Bears are large mammals of the family Ursidae, found across North America, South America, Europe, and Asia. With eight living species, bears have adapted to an impressive range of habitats, from arctic ice to tropical forests, from sea level to mountain peaks above 5,000 meters.

The largest bears are the polar bear and the Kodiak brown bear, both of which can exceed 700 kilograms. At the other end of the scale, the sun bear of Southeast Asia weighs as little as 25 kilograms. Despite their bulk, bears are surprisingly agile — grizzlies can sprint at 55 kilometers per hour, and black bears are accomplished tree climbers.

Most bears are omnivores with remarkably varied diets. Brown bears in Alaska gorge on salmon during spawning runs, but also eat berries, roots, insects, and grasses. The spectacled bear of South America feeds primarily on fruit and bromeliads. The giant panda has famously specialized in bamboo, while the polar bear is almost entirely carnivorous, hunting seals on sea ice.

Hibernation is one of the bear's most impressive adaptations. During winter dormancy, a bear's heart rate can drop from 40 beats per minute to as few as 8, and its body temperature decreases only slightly. The bear does not eat, drink, urinate, or defecate for months, surviving entirely on stored fat. Remarkably, female bears give birth during hibernation, nursing their tiny cubs while still in their winter dens.
