Octopuses are soft-bodied mollusks belonging to the order Octopoda, with around 300 recognized species inhabiting every ocean on Earth. Despite being invertebrates — more closely related to snails than to fish — octopuses display a level of intelligence that rivals many vertebrates.

The octopus brain is remarkably complex, containing about 500 million neurons, roughly two-thirds of which are distributed throughout the arms rather than centralized in the head. This means each arm can taste, touch, and even make basic decisions independently. An octopus can open a jar with one arm, investigate an object with another, and camouflage the rest of its body simultaneously.

Camouflage in octopuses is unmatched in the animal kingdom. Specialized skin cells allow them to change color, pattern, and even texture in milliseconds, perfectly mimicking rocks, coral, sand, or algae. The mimic octopus takes this further, actively impersonating other animals like lionfish, flatfish, and sea snakes to deter predators.

Octopuses are solitary and short-lived, with most species surviving only one to two years. Females of many species make the ultimate sacrifice, guarding their eggs without eating for months until they hatch, then dying of exhaustion. Despite their brief lives, octopuses demonstrate remarkable learning abilities, solving complex puzzles, using tools, and even recognizing individual humans — capabilities that continue to challenge our understanding of how intelligence evolves.
