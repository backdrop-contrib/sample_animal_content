The gray wolf is the largest wild member of the dog family, with a range that once spanned most of the Northern Hemisphere. Wolves are highly social animals that live in family groups called packs, typically consisting of a breeding pair and their offspring from the current and previous years.

Pack structure is the foundation of wolf society. Contrary to popular belief, wolf packs are not led by aggressive alpha males fighting for dominance. Instead, they function much like human families, with the breeding pair guiding the group and older siblings helping to raise younger pups. This cooperative structure allows wolves to hunt prey far larger than themselves, including elk, moose, and bison.

Wolves communicate through an elaborate system of vocalizations, body language, and scent marking. Their iconic howl serves multiple purposes: rallying the pack before a hunt, advertising territory to rival packs, and strengthening social bonds. Each wolf's howl is unique, and pack members can identify individuals by voice alone.

The ecological importance of wolves became dramatically clear when they were reintroduced to Yellowstone National Park in 1995. Their return triggered a trophic cascade that transformed the entire ecosystem — elk changed their grazing patterns, allowing streamside vegetation to recover, which stabilized riverbanks, cooled water temperatures, and brought back species that had declined in the wolves' absence.
