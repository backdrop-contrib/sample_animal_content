Sharks have patrolled the world's oceans for over 400 million years, predating dinosaurs by roughly 200 million years. These cartilaginous fish have survived five mass extinction events, evolving into one of the most successful and diverse groups of predators on the planet.

More than 500 species of shark exist today, ranging from the dwarf lanternshark at just 20 centimeters to the whale shark, which can grow to over 12 meters and is the largest fish in the sea. Despite their fearsome reputation, the vast majority of shark species are harmless to humans, and many feed on plankton or small fish.

Sharks possess an extraordinary array of senses. They can detect electrical fields generated by the muscles and nerves of other animals using specialized organs called ampullae of Lorenzini. Their sense of smell is legendary — some species can detect a single drop of blood in an Olympic swimming pool. They also have excellent low-light vision and can sense vibrations in the water from kilometers away.

As apex predators, sharks are essential to ocean health. They regulate prey populations, remove sick and weak individuals, and maintain the balance of marine food webs. When shark populations decline, the effects cascade through entire ecosystems. Yet sharks face severe threats from overfishing, with an estimated 100 million killed each year, many for their fins alone.
