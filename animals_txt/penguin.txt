Penguins are flightless seabirds found almost exclusively in the Southern Hemisphere. While most people associate them with Antarctica, penguins actually inhabit a wide range of environments, from the icy shores of the subantarctic islands to the tropical Galapagos.

There are 18 recognized species of penguin, varying dramatically in size. The emperor penguin stands over a meter tall and weighs up to 45 kilograms, while the little blue penguin of Australia and New Zealand barely reaches 30 centimeters. Despite their differences, all penguins share the same basic body plan: a torpedo-shaped body, dense waterproof feathers, and wings modified into powerful flippers.

Penguins are supremely adapted to life in the water. They can dive to remarkable depths — emperor penguins have been recorded at over 500 meters — and hold their breath for more than 20 minutes. Their black and white coloring serves as camouflage: from above, their dark backs blend with the ocean depths, while from below, their white bellies match the bright surface.

Most penguin species are highly social, forming colonies that can number in the hundreds of thousands. Their cooperative breeding behaviors, including the emperor penguin's famous habit of huddling together for warmth during the Antarctic winter, are among the most studied examples of social behavior in the animal kingdom.
