Frogs are amphibians belonging to the order Anura, a group that has thrived on Earth for over 200 million years. With more than 7,000 known species, frogs are among the most diverse vertebrates, inhabiting every continent except Antarctica.

Frogs undergo one of nature's most dramatic transformations. Beginning life as aquatic, gill-breathing tadpoles, they gradually develop lungs, grow legs, absorb their tails, and emerge onto land as air-breathing adults. This metamorphosis can take anywhere from a few weeks to several years depending on the species.

The diversity of frogs is astonishing. The golden poison frog of Colombia carries enough toxin on its skin to kill ten adult humans, while the tiny Paedophryne amauensis of Papua New Guinea measures just 7.7 millimeters — the smallest known vertebrate. The wood frog of North America can survive being frozen solid during winter, its heart stopping and its blood crystallizing before thawing back to life in spring.

Frogs play a critical ecological role as both predator and prey. Adults consume enormous quantities of insects, including many agricultural pests, while tadpoles help control algae in freshwater systems. Unfortunately, frogs are facing a global crisis: a fungal disease called chytridiomycosis, combined with habitat loss and climate change, has driven dozens of species to extinction and threatens hundreds more.
